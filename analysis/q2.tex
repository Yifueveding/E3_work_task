\section*{Inputs and Notation}

All revenue is calculated from hourly 2023 NYISO N.Y.C. zone day-ahead prices.
For hour $t$, let $p_t$ be the day-ahead energy price (\$/MWh) and $s_t$ be the
normalized solar production shape. Solar output is assumed to be available for
the full hour, so MW and MWh are numerically equivalent for each hourly interval.

\centering

\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Solar size & 300 MW-DC \\
Solar inverter limit & 250 MW-AC \\
Storage power capacity & 50 MW \\
Storage energy capacity & 200 MWh \\
Storage duration & $200\text{ MWh} / 50\text{ MW} = 4$ hours \\
Round-trip efficiency & 100\% per provided input \\
Dispatch & Per-day LP optimum (multi-cycle), perfect day-ahead foresight \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{Note on price basis:} we use day-ahead (DA) prices specifically because the
storage dispatch model (Q2b/Q2c) assumes perfect foresight of which hours to charge and
discharge within a day --- an assumption that is realistic under DA prices, since they are
actually published and known before the operating day, but would not be realistic under
real-time (RT) prices, which are not knowable in advance. A parallel RT-basis run was also
completed for comparison: it earns modestly more (solar \$18.91M, storage \$2.73M) because
it implicitly credits the model with foreknowledge of prices an RT-only strategy would not
actually have in advance --- the DA-basis figures below are the more defensible base case.

\section*{Q2a. Standalone Solar Revenue}

Hourly solar generation is calculated from the normalized production shape and
capped at the inverter limit:

\[
G_t = \min(s_t \times 300\text{ MW},\ 250\text{ MW})
\]

Annual backcast solar revenue is the sum of hourly delivered energy times the
day-ahead price:

\[
R_{\text{solar}} = \sum_{t=1}^{8760} G_t \times p_t
\]

Using the 2023 hourly data:

\[
\sum_t G_t = 551{,}454\text{ MWh}
\]

\[
R_{\text{solar}} = \$18{,}803{,}819
\]

\[
\text{Solar capture price}
= \frac{\$18{,}803{,}819}{551{,}454\text{ MWh}}
= \$34.10/\text{MWh}
\]

The inverter cap clips 372 MWh, equal to 0.07\% of gross solar generation, so
clipping has a negligible effect on standalone solar revenue.

\section*{Q2b. Standalone Storage Revenue}

Storage is modeled as daily energy arbitrage, but rather than assume a single fixed
4-hour charge / 4-hour discharge cycle, each day $d$ is solved as its own linear program:
choose hourly charge $c_h \geq 0$ and discharge $g_h \geq 0$ (both $\leq 50$ MW) to

\[
\max \sum_{h \in d} p_h (g_h - c_h)
\]

subject to, for every hour $h$ in day $d$:

\[
0 \ \leq\ \sum_{h' \leq h,\ h' \in d} (c_{h'} - g_{h'}) \ \leq\ 200\text{ MWh}
\]

i.e. the running state of charge must stay within the battery's 200 MWh energy limit
at every hour, starting the day at zero, with 100\% round-trip efficiency (per the given
input, so no loss term). This lets the optimizer discover \emph{multiple} charge/discharge
cycles within a day when day-ahead prices oscillate enough to make it profitable, rather
than assuming exactly one cycle.

Inspecting individual days confirms this is a real effect, not a solver artifact. For
example, April 11 has two separate profitable round trips across the day (an overnight
dip into a morning peak, and a midday dip into an evening peak) --- the optimizer uses 8
charge hours and 8 discharge hours that day, double a single 4-hour/4-hour cycle, because
day-ahead prices simply have more usable volatility in a day than one high/low pair can
capture.

Annual standalone storage revenue is:

\[
R_{\text{storage}}
= \sum_{d=1}^{365} R_{\text{storage},d}
= \$1{,}977{,}954
\]

Normalized by installed capacity:

\[
\frac{\$1{,}977{,}954}{50{,}000\text{ kW}}
= \$39.56/\text{kW-year}
\]

\[
\frac{\$1{,}977{,}954}{200{,}000\text{ kWh}}
= \$9.89/\text{kWh-year}
\]

\textbf{Caveat:} this still assumes zero degradation/cycling cost, so additional cycling
is ``free'' here --- a real operator would cycle less than this LP optimum once a
per-cycle degradation cost is included (Q3b). Treat this as an upper bound on what
multi-cycle dispatch can add, not a realistic operating recommendation on its own.

\section*{Q2c. Combined Solar + Storage Revenue}

The combined case keeps the same solar export calculation as standalone solar. Storage
uses the same per-day LP as Q2b, except each hour's charging can first draw up to
$\min(C_t, 50\text{ MW})$ for \$0 from that hour's otherwise-clipped solar,

\[
C_t = \max(s_t \times 300\text{ MW} - 250\text{ MW},\ 0)
\]

before any additional charging that hour is priced at the grid rate --- the optimizer
naturally prefers the \$0 option first since it strictly lowers cost. Discharge and the
multi-cycle structure are otherwise identical to Q2b. Across 2023, the model uses:

\[
\sum_t \min(C_t, 50) = 372\text{ MWh (free)}, \qquad
\text{grid-charged} = 125{,}595\text{ MWh}
\]

The resulting combined revenues are:

\[
R_{\text{combined solar}} = \$18{,}803{,}819
\]

\[
R_{\text{combined storage}} = \$1{,}987{,}978
\]

\[
R_{\text{combined total}}
= \$18{,}803{,}819 + \$1{,}987{,}978
= \$20{,}791{,}797
\]

The interaction benefit versus building standalone solar and standalone storage
separately is:

\[
R_{\text{combined total}} -
\left(R_{\text{solar}} + R_{\text{storage}}\right)
= \$20{,}791{,}797 - (\$18{,}803{,}819 + \$1{,}977{,}954)
= \$10{,}023
\]

\[
\frac{\$10{,}023}{\$20{,}781{,}773}
= 0.048\%
\]

\section*{Q2d. Reserve (Headroom/Footroom) Scenario --- Extension Beyond the Base Exercise}

As a further scenario, we estimate additional revenue each configuration could earn by
offering NYISO operating reserve capacity, at an assumed reserve price of \$1.25/MW-hr
(midpoint of the \$1--1.5/MW range). \emph{Headroom} is capacity available to increase
output on call (up-reserve); \emph{footroom} is capacity available to decrease output on
call (down-reserve).

Solar cannot offer reserve at all. It is a variable, weather-dependent resource with no
firm or dispatchable capacity commitment, so it does not qualify to provide NYISO reserve
products --- standalone solar earns \$0 in reserve revenue:

\[
R_{\text{solar reserve}} = \$0
\]

Storage's reserve capacity is classified hour-by-hour from the actual Q2b/Q2c LP
dispatch solution: an hour where the LP charges (and doesn't discharge) offers 50 MW of
footroom only; an hour where it discharges (and doesn't charge) offers 50 MW of headroom
only; an idle hour offers the full 50 MW of \emph{both} headroom and footroom
simultaneously:

\[
R_{\text{storage reserve}} = \$1.25 \times (312{,}550 + 312{,}550)\text{ MW-hr} = \$781{,}375
\]

In the combined system, all reserve capacity comes from the storage component only ---
the solar component still cannot offer reserve --- using the same hour-by-hour
classification applied to the combined dispatch:

\[
R_{\text{combined reserve}} = \$1.25 \times (312{,}550 + 312{,}950)\text{ MW-hr} = \$781{,}875
\]

Storage is the only source of reserve value here (+39.5\% on a standalone basis) because
its reserve capacity comes free alongside energy dispatch, and idle hours can stack both
headroom and footroom at the same time. Solar contributes nothing, since it cannot commit
firm capacity to a reserve product --- so the combined system's uplift (+3.8\%) is diluted
relative to standalone storage's, simply because storage is a small share of the combined
system's total revenue. Note the direct tension with Q2b: multi-cycle dispatch earns more
energy revenue precisely by keeping the battery busy charging or discharging more of the
day, which leaves fewer idle hours available to sell as reserve --- a real operator would
face a genuine energy-vs-reserve tradeoff that this model does not co-optimize. This
scenario is illustrative: real NYISO reserve products have stricter response-time,
minimum-run, and co-optimization rules than modeled here, and a resource cannot literally
be paid full price for headroom and footroom at the same instant if doing so double-counts
the same physical capacity across products --- a more rigorous treatment is one of the
natural extensions noted in Q3b.
